The Album

Recorded in New York in 1964 for Philips, Broadway-Blues-Ballads arrived as Nina Simone was pushing against the narrow "jazz singer" tag the industry kept fastening on her, and the record's deliberate mix of Broadway show tunes, blues-inflected material, art song and her own compositions was itself an argument for her refusal to be confined to one idiom. Produced and largely arranged by Hal Mooney, with additional charts by Horace Ott, the album ranges from a blazing take on "Don't Let Me Be Misunderstood" (a song the Animals would soon turn into a hit, though Simone had introduced it first) to Rodgers and Hammerstein's "Something Wonderful" and Cole Porter's "The Laziest Gal in Town," alongside her own stark, African-inflected "See-Line Woman." By this point Simone was several albums into her Philips contract and increasingly using her records as a platform for artistic and social statement rather than pure entertainment, a trajectory that would flower fully in the explicitly political recordings of the mid-1960s that followed. The album is emblematic of her genre-defiant catalog and is frequently cited by critics and biographers as a key document of her restless stylistic range during this period. It has endured less as a single unified concept than as a showcase for the breadth of her interpretive gifts, and songs from it - especially "Don't Let Me Be Misunderstood" and "See-Line Woman" - have had a long afterlife well beyond the original LP, cemented in her reputation as one of American music's most singular and uncompromising vocalists.

The Label

The side labels are a grey/silver field with a white-on-grey "PHILIPS" shield crest (wave-and-stars logo) and a broad multicolour rainbow band (violet through orange) running horizontally through the spindle area - the standard US Philips "rainbow" stereo label used c.1962-67 - with STEREO printed at left and PHS-600-148 at lower left. The back cover carries "Philips Records, A Division of Mercury Record Productions, Inc. - Vendor: Mercury Record Corporation," the distribution wording used while Philips product was still handled through Mercury in the US, and Discogs' matching stereo variant (release 5762851) documents this same rainbow label with the identical "Vendor: Mercury" back-cover text as its first US stereo pressing. Combined with the deep-groove pressing on this copy, that places it at the head of the US stereo hierarchy for this title, ahead of any later black Philips label or reissue.

Fidelity

This is an original 1964 Philips analog cut, mastered for the domestic US stereo release with a lacquer stamped RR (pressing-plant/mastering code) rather than a later remaster; there is no Van Gelder-style engineer credit documented for this pop-label session. Collector discussion of the title (Discogs user comments, general vinyl forums) is thin and mostly concerns modern 180-gram repress copies, which listeners describe as reasonably balanced but sometimes soft in the low end compared to vintage pressings, with no dedicated Steve Hoffman thread found specifically comparing original stereo vs. mono vs. reissue cuts for this title. Within the album's fidelity hierarchy, this deep-groove US stereo first pressing sits at or near the top alongside the mono first pressing (collectors debate which channel configuration is preferable for Mooney's orchestral arrangements), clearly ahead of the Australian and UK pressings and well ahead of the 2010s-2020s 180-gram reissue, which trades vintage analog warmth for modern quiet surfaces.

Notes

1964 Philips PHS 600-148 stereo - original US first pressing. The grey/rainbow Philips label with deep groove and the "Philips Records...Vendor: Mercury Record Corporation" back-cover credit match Discogs' documented first stereo variant (release 5762851), whose transcribed runouts (PHS600148A-M1 || B RR / PHS600148B-M1 | RR C) correspond closely to this copy's typed matrix (PHS 600148A-M1 RR / PHS 600148B-M1 RR), differing only in stamper suffix letters not captured in the typed transcription. No promo, club, or reissue markers are present, confirming this as a standard retail first pressing rather than the pink-label mono promo variant also sold from this era.
