The Album

Know What I Mean? brought together Cannonball Adderley's working rhythm section of Percy Heath and Connie Kay with pianist Bill Evans for a one-off studio reunion, their first joint session since Evans had left Miles Davis's band and Adderley had gone on to lead his own hard-bop quartets and quintets. The pairing let Adderley, an exuberant, blues-rooted alto saxophonist, explore a gentler, more harmonically reflective setting shaped by Evans's impressionistic touch, with a program built from ballads and standards rather than the hard-driving originals typical of Adderley's working group. The session produced an early reading of Evans's own Waltz for Debby alongside pieces by Clifford Jordan, Earl Zindars and others, with Evans contributing the title track as well. It arrived during a particularly fertile period for both musicians - Adderley at the height of his commercial and artistic success on Riverside, Evans establishing himself as one of the most influential pianists in jazz - and the album has long been prized by critics and collectors as a rare, intimate crossing of their two musical worlds. Its relaxed, lyrical character distinguishes it within Adderley's catalogue and it remains regarded as one of the most rewarding latter-day studio pairings of the Miles Davis alumni.

The Label

This copy's labels are the white Original Jazz Classics reproduction of the mid-1950s Riverside design: a pale grey-blue RIVERSIDE banner over the Riverside reels (side 1 a single reels logo, side 2 the reels-and-tape-head device), black/dark-grey print, and the Fantasy, Inc., Berkeley, California rim line rather than any New York Riverside address. Fantasy acquired the Riverside catalogue and began reissuing it under its Original Jazz Classics imprint beginning in the early 1980s, replicating the old Riverside label art on new white OJC stock while keeping the original catalogue number in parentheses. That places this pressing squarely as a 1980s OJC reissue of the 1962 Riverside stereo original, not an original-era Riverside pressing itself - the label's own reproduction design and OJC catalogue prefix rule out a first-pressing placement regardless of how closely the artwork mimics the 1950s original.

Fidelity

This OJC pressing was cut from the Riverside master tapes recorded at Bell Sound Studios and manufactured at the Columbia Pitman plant, a high-volume but generally reliable pressing facility used throughout Fantasy's early-1980s OJC reissue series. Collectors on the forums regard these OJC pressings as solid, musically faithful reissues that capture most of the warmth of the originals, though they are consistently ranked below the 1962 Riverside stereo first pressing and below the later audiophile 180-gram and 45rpm remasterings of this title in outright transparency and dynamic range. Within the reissue tier itself, this standard-weight OJC pressing sits in the middle of the fidelity hierarchy for the title - above budget foreign pressings but below the more recent heavyweight vinyl editions.

Notes

This is the 1984 Fantasy/Original Jazz Classics reissue of the 1962 Riverside stereo LP, pressed at the Columbia Records plant in Pitman as the etched "P" in the runout denotes. The white reproduction Riverside labels with the Fantasy, Inc., Berkeley, California rim text and the OJC-105 (RLP-9433) catalogue pairing identify it firmly as an early-1980s OJC pressing rather than an original Riverside issue. The runout etching GH/P OJC 105 A-1 and B-1 on both sides matches the catalogue numbers used for this particular OJC pressing run.
