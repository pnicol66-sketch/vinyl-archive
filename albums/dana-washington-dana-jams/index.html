The Album

Recorded in a single evening on August 14, 1954 at a Hollywood studio before an invited audience, this session grew out of producer Bob Shad's plan to pair Dinah Washington, already a star of the rhythm-and-blues circuit, with the modern jazz musicians then working the West Coast clubs. The core of the horn section was drawn from the Clifford Brown-Max Roach quintet, newly formed and about to become one of the defining hard-bop groups of the decade, with Max Roach's drums and Richie Powell's piano anchoring the rhythm section while Maynard Ferguson, Clark Terry and Herb Geller sat in for extended stretches. Washington had spent most of the previous decade scoring blues and R&B hits for Mercury, and this loosely structured, jam-session format let her stand alongside first-rank jazz improvisers on standards rather than the shorter commercial sides that had built her reputation, a pairing that both tested and confirmed her standing as a jazz singer. The extended, multi-song medley that fills side one, built around "Alone Together," "Summertime" and "Come Rain or Come Shine," gave the horns room to stretch far beyond the three-minute single, with Clifford Brown's solo work drawing particular notice for its combination of technical control and melodic invention in the final months before his death in 1956. Critics and later listeners have singled the album out as a high point in Washington's catalogue precisely because it catches her trading phrases with musicians of Brown's and Roach's caliber on equal footing, and it is remembered today as one of the era's landmark vocal-jazz jam sessions, still cited for the chemistry between Washington's blues-rooted phrasing and the quintet's hard-bop drive.

The Label

Both sides are deep-groove labels: blue and white, with the EmArcy script in an oval at upper left and the drummer figure to its right, the rim printing LONG PLAYING HIGH FIDELITY over MERCURY RECORD CORPORATION MADE IN U.S.A. This drummer-logo deep-groove design is the earliest EmArcy jazz-series label, used from the line's 1954 launch through the mid-1950s before the later yellow/maroon and Mercury-owned label redesigns. A deep-groove pressing with this label design places the copy at the front of the catalogue number's pressing history, ahead of the flat-label, laminated-jacket reissue that followed in 1958. The label prints the catalogue number as MG 36000 A/B while the jacket prints only MG 36000, a minor and immaterial discrepancy between the two surfaces.

Fidelity

The session was cut using Mercury's Fine-Fairchild Margin Control mastering process, noted on the jacket as a method aimed at wider dynamic range and more reliable stylus tracking for the period; the recording itself is a single-microphone mono live-audience capture with no stereo source, so no stereo remix exists for this title. Collectors generally rate the original EmArcy mono pressings as the most natural-sounding version of this session, prizing the immediacy of the horns and Washington's voice against the more compressed later reissues and the digitally remastered CD editions. Among the later vinyl reissues, the Japanese EmArcy pressings are regarded as closer in character to the original mono cut than the 1970s-80s U.S. Trip/EmArcy reissues, but none supersede a clean original deep-groove pressing like this one.

Notes

The deep-groove drummer-logo EmArcy label on both sides, the matte (unlaminated) jacket, and the green-lettered front cover place this copy in the earliest 1955 pressing window for the catalogue number, ahead of the 1958 laminated-jacket reissue. The transcribed runout suffixes (M E on side one, M A on side two) differ from the G suffix recorded for the matched release, which points to another stamper pairing from the same original pressing run rather than a later edition - the deep-groove ring and label design are the more decisive first-pressing tells here. No pressing plant is named on the label itself beyond "Mercury Record Corporation, Made in U.S.A."
