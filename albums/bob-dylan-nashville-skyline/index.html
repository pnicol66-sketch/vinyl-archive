The Album

Recorded in Nashville in the wake of Dylan's motorcycle-accident retreat and his increasingly rustic, song-craft-focused turn with The Band, this record found him singing in a warmer, lower register and leaning fully into the country idiom he had only hinted at before. Cut quickly with Nashville session veterans, the album pairs spare, direct love songs with instrumental and novelty pieces, closing a trilogy of post-accident reinvention that began with the stripped-down basement recordings and the plainspoken writing of the album that preceded it. The duet with Johnny Cash that opens the record cemented a long mutual admiration between the two artists and signaled, to a rock audience still processing his electric years, that Dylan now stood at the crossroads of rock and country music. Its brevity and lack of overt protest or surrealism stunned some critics while delighting others, and it is now seen as a pivotal moment in the country-rock movement, opening the door for a wave of rock artists to record in Nashville and draw on its musicians and sound. The album's warmth and simplicity marked one of the most unexpected stylistic pivots of Dylan's career, and tracks from it remain among his most covered and best-loved love songs.

The Label

The labels are red, with a Columbia box logo at either side of the rim and a 'STEREO'/'360 SOUND' arc text running around the bottom edge; the catalogue number KCS 9825 sits at left and the side/track listing at right. The labels show no deep groove ring. This red '360 Sound' twin-logo design is the stereo Columbia style used across the label's late-1960s catalogue, with printed credits set in a plain sans-serif upper-case face typical of Columbia's contemporary stock; the pressing plant is not identified on the label. That design places the copy within the late-1960s/early-1970s stereo Columbia era as an original-era US stereo pressing, without a further printed marker fixing it to one specific first-state printing within that window.

Fidelity

The album was cut from the original Nashville session master tapes for Columbia's contemporary stereo pressing chain, with no indication of a later remix or remaster on this copy's labels or runout. Collectors generally rate the original-era Columbia pressings of this title as sonically direct and uncomplicated, prizing them mainly for historical authenticity rather than audiophile spectacle, since the recording itself is intentionally spare. Later audiophile and 180-gram reissues have since appeared and are valued for quieter vinyl, but the early Columbia stereo pressings such as this one remain the reference point collectors compare those reissues against.
