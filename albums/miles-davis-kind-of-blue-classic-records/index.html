The Album

Recorded in two sessions at Columbia's 30th Street Studio in New York on March 2 and April 22, 1959, Kind of Blue grew out of Miles Davis's interest in modal composition, an approach he had been exploring with pianist and co-writer Bill Evans; Davis brought the band only sketches of scales and melodic frameworks rather than fully notated charts, pushing the septet to improvise within simple modal structures instead of dense chord changes. The septet united Davis with John Coltrane, Julian "Cannonball" Adderley, Bill Evans (with Wynton Kelly sitting in for "Freddie Freeloader"), Paul Chambers, and Jimmy Cobb, a lineup that let two contrasting saxophone voices and two pianists color the same modal palette from different angles. Arriving at the height of Davis's late-1950s creative surge, the album marked a turning point away from the harmonic density of bebop toward the spacious, scale-based language that would influence jazz, rock, and beyond for decades. Its unhurried tempos, blues-rooted melodies, and largely first-take performances captured a collective spontaneity that Bill Evans himself likened to a painter working directly and without revision, an ethos he described in his own liner note for the LP. Kind of Blue is widely regarded as the best-selling and most influential jazz album ever made, frequently cited as the essential entry point to the genre and a permanent fixture on best-album lists across all styles of music. Its legacy rests not only on its commercial reach but on how thoroughly modal improvisation, as introduced here, reshaped the vocabulary of jazz soloing for generations of musicians that followed.

The Label

This copy carries plain white RTI (Record Technology Incorporated) 'TEST PRESSING' labels rubber-stamped 'APR 2 1997' with matrix 'CS 8163 A1/B1', identifying it as a pre-release test pressing struck at RTI for Classic Records' audiophile reissue of Kind of Blue, not a retail-label copy. Classic Records licensed the Columbia/Sony catalog and issued CS 8163 on 200-gram Quiex SV-P vinyl beginning in the mid-1990s, with Bernie Grundman recutting lacquers directly from the original three-track analog masters; the jacket used for the eventual retail run bears a 2001 Sony/Classic copyright line, but the test-pressing date shows this specific stamper/lacquer pair (the 'BG' matrix suffix) was cut in April 1997, ahead of that later commercial pressing cycle. Because RTI test pressings are struck in very small quantities purely for quality-control approval before a title is approved for the full run, this places the disc at the very top of the Classic Records variant hierarchy for CS 8163, above the routine 200g retail copies that followed. The plain jacket insert (blank white, stamped identically to the label) confirms it left the plant as a test unit rather than a packaged consumer product.

Fidelity

The lacquers were cut by Bernie Grundman directly from the original three-track analog session masters with no additional EQ, on Classic Records' all-tube cutting chain, and pressed at RTI on audiophile-grade vinyl for the retail run; this test pressing represents that same Grundman cut at its earliest, pre-approval stage. Steve Hoffman forum consensus generally rates the Classic Records Kind of Blue highly among vintage-tape-sourced reissues, praising its openness and low-level detail compared to standard CBS/Columbia pressings, though some prefer later 45rpm cuts (Analogue Productions/QRP) for outright resolution. Within the album's full fidelity hierarchy this sits below the original 1959 six-eye Columbia lacquers (prized for tape freshness) and below the 2020s Analogue Productions 'Corrected Speed' 45rpm QRP pressing (considered the current audiophile benchmark), but above the routine 1970s-80s Columbia reissues and most CD/digital editions; as a test pressing it also typically offers the cleanest, most consistent playback surface from that stamper pair before any commercial wear-and-tear on the plating.
