The Album

Aretha Franklin was twenty-one when she cut this set at Columbia's New York and Hollywood studios in April and June of 1963, three years after John Hammond signed the Detroit preacher's daughter on the strength of a voice formed in her father's New Bethel Baptist Church. Columbia's answer to that voice was to frame it as a supper-club jazz singer, and here the framing is handed to Robert Mersey, the staff arranger behind Andy Williams, Johnny Mathis and Barbra Streisand, who produced the date as well as writing and conducting the charts. The programme is almost all standards - Carmichael and Mercer's Skylark, Ellington's Solitude, Berlin's Say It Isn't So, Lerner and Loewe's If Ever I Would Leave You from Camelot - with one original, I Wonder (Where Are You Tonight), written by Franklin herself with Ted White, and the sly Mr. Ugly as the set's one piece of grit. Where the arrangements settle into velvet, Franklin keeps pushing against it: the gospel phrasing, the sudden held notes and the melismatic turns are already the instrument that would detonate at Atlantic four years later. Contemporary writers placed her in the line of Bessie Smith and Billie Holiday, and the trade press had just voted her the new star female vocalist in a major critics' poll. The Columbia records were long dismissed as the wrong clothes on the right singer, but this one is regularly picked out as the strongest of them, Make Someone Happy and Skylark in particular standing as evidence that the jazz-ballad Aretha was no mere rehearsal. It remains the essential document of what she could do with the Great American Songbook before soul claimed her entirely.

The Label

Red Columbia two-eye label: COLUMBIA arched in white across the top, a walking-eye logo in a white-outlined box at 9 o'clock and at 3 o'clock, black centre print reading CL 2079 / XLP 75207 (side 1) and XLP 75208 (side 2), and MONO in large white letters at the foot with no “Guaranteed High Fidelity” line and no “360 Sound” arcs or arrows; the rim prints ®“COLUMBIA”, MARCAS REG. PRINTED IN U.S.A. with a small eye logo in the foot text. Columbia's two-eye design arrived in mid-1962 with “Guaranteed High Fidelity” at the foot of its mono labels, replaced that line with “360 SOUND” and arrows in August 1965, and in April 1967 dropped both for the single word MONO - the last mono label the company printed before going stereo-only in 1968. Side 2 carries a deep-groove label, a die still in use at the plant that pressed it. The plain MONO foot therefore places this copy in the 1967-68 mono window, a late repress of a 1963 album and a step below the 1963 “Guaranteed High Fidelity” two-eye first pressing; the stereo counterpart of the same era carries the 360 Sound arrows instead.

Fidelity

A Columbia in-house mono cut pressed at Terre Haute, side 1 from a 1D lacquer and side 2 from the 1C used on the first pressings, with the plant's standard late-sixties vinyl. Mersey's orchestra sits warm and close behind the voice in the dedicated mono mix, which collectors prefer to the 1963 stereo for its weight and centred vocal. Terre Haute pressings of this period are rated a shade below the quieter Pitman surfaces, and this later cut sits behind the 1963 mono first pressing in the title's fidelity order while staying well ahead of the thin European reissues of the last decade.

Notes

Columbia CL 2079 mono, a late-1960s repress of the August 1963 album. The red two-eye labels carry the single word MONO in white at the foot, without the “Guaranteed High Fidelity” line of the 1963 first pressing or the “360 Sound” arrows used from August 1965, a foot line Columbia adopted in April 1967 and kept until mono ended in 1968. The runout reads XLP-75207-1D 0 on side 1 and XLP-75208-1C 0 T on side 2, the T marking the Terre Haute plant and the 1D lacquer on side 1 a recut later than the 1C pair used in 1963. Side 2 has a deep-groove label, side 1 a flat one. The jacket is the original 1963 artwork, printed MONAURAL-CL 2079 at the top left and GUARANTEED HIGH FIDELITY at the foot.
