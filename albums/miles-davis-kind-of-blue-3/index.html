This Pressing

Early six-eye mono pressing (Columbia CL 1355) - deep groove label, XLP47324-1B / XLP47325-1C runout stampers, an early but not the absolute first (-1A/-1A) stamper generation.

The Album

Recorded at Columbia's 30th Street Studio in New York across two sessions in March and April 1959, Kind of Blue grew out of Miles Davis's growing interest in modal composition, an approach he had begun testing on "Milestones" the year before, trading dense chord changes for simple scalar frameworks that opened up space for improvisation. Davis reportedly brought the band only brief sketches rather than fully written charts, asking his sidemen to improvise around scales and moods encountered largely for the first time in the studio, a method the original liner notes by pianist Bill Evans compared to the direct, unrevisable brushwork of Japanese ink painting. The sextet assembled for the date - Davis, John Coltrane, Cannonball Adderley, Bill Evans, Paul Chambers, and Jimmy Cobb, with Wynton Kelly sitting in for "Freddie Freeloader" - represented an extraordinary concentration of talent, several of whom would soon lead their own historically important groups. The album arrived at a pivotal juncture in Davis's career, following his hard-bop and cool-toned work for Prestige and early Columbia, and it redirected jazz improvisation toward modality just as Ornette Coleman and others pushed the music toward free forms by a different route. Kind of Blue is widely regarded as the best-selling and most influential jazz album ever made, cited by generations of musicians across jazz, rock, and classical music as a foundational listening experience, and it remains a fixture of best-albums lists and jazz history curricula. Its restrained, spacious lyricism - epitomized by "So What" and "Blue in Green" - demonstrated that jazz improvisation could be harmonically simple and emotionally profound at once, cementing the album's legacy as a touchstone of twentieth-century American music.

The Label

The label photos show Columbia's red "six-eye" design (six small "eye" logos around the rim, "Guaranteed High Fidelity" text at the top of the label field, deep groove visible around the spindle hole, and "NONBREAKABLE" printed under the catalog number) on both sides - a design Columbia used roughly 1958 into the early 1960s before switching to flat-edge (non-deep-groove) six-eye labels and, later, the 1968+ "360 Sound" text. The runout stampers, XLP47324-1B (Side 1) and XLP47325-1C (Side 2), are documented by LondonJazzCollector's matrix study as among the earliest lacquer generations used on the commercial mono release (1A/1B/1C being the earliest letters found), placing this copy just behind the true first -1A/-1A pairing. Combined, the deep-groove six-eye label and low-letter stampers place this copy firmly in the 1959-1961 first-issue manufacturing window, ahead of the later flat-edge six-eye pressings and far ahead of the 360 Sound reissues.

Fidelity

The mono mix of Kind of Blue is generally preferred by collectors and engineers over the original stereo master, since the stereo reels were affected by a tape machine running slightly fast/slow during the sessions, a flaw largely sidestepped on the mono tape; this six-eye mono pressing sits near the top of the album's fidelity hierarchy for that reason. Early six-eye Columbia pressings from this era used relatively high-quality lacquers and pressings by Columbia's own plants, and Steve Hoffman forum consensus generally rates original six-eye mono copies (especially early-stamper ones like -1B/-1C) as warmer and more three-dimensional than later reissues. Later benchmarks include the 1997 Mark Wilder Columbia/Legacy remaster, the 2009 Sony Legacy 50th-anniversary mono LP reissue (cut from analog tape), and the Analogue Productions/Classic Records audiophile stereo reissues; most forum listeners still rank an early six-eye mono original like this one above those later cuts for tonal warmth, even though the later audiophile stereo pressings offer superior surface noise and channel separation.

Notes

This is an early Columbia six-eye mono first-issue pressing of CL 1355, not a later reissue. Deep groove labels, the "Guaranteed High Fidelity" six-eye design, and the "NONBREAKABLE" rim text all point to manufacture in the 1959-1961 window, before Columbia dropped the deep groove and years before the 1968 switch to "360 Sound" labels. The runout stampers, -1B on Side 1 and -1C on Side 2, indicate an early stamper generation close to (but one or two removed from) the true first -1A/-1A pairing prized by specialists. The jacket's misspelling of "Cannonball" Adderley's surname as "Adderly" and drummer Jimmy Cobb's name as "James Cobb" are consistent with the original-issue back cover text used on early copies of this title.
