The Album

Charles Lloyd arrived at this session already marked as jazz's newest and most closely watched young star, having built his reputation in Chico Hamilton's group and then as musical director of the Cannonball Adderley Sextet before striking out as a leader. For this quartet date he assembled a remarkably young and forward-looking band - guitarist Gabor Szabo, bassist Ron Carter and drummer Tony Williams, both the latter on loan from Miles Davis's group - and the unusual addition of guitar in place of piano gave the record a lighter, more open harmonic texture that let Lloyd's tenor and flute roam freely. The material mixes Lloyd originals with standards by Irving Berlin and by Styne and Cahn, and several of the originals, including the blues-drenched closer, reach back to Lloyd's teenage years playing Memphis blues alongside musicians like B.B. King and Bobby Bland. The quartet's deep musical rapport - described by Lloyd himself as a four-way conversation in which each player anticipates and spurs the others - gives the album an unusually spontaneous, conversational feel even on the more composed pieces. Coming on the heels of his acclaimed debut as a leader, this album consolidated Lloyd's standing as a leading voice of the new jazz mainstream, one capable of bridging hard-bop roots with the freer, more exploratory language emerging in the mid-1960s. It remains a touchstone of Lloyd's early-to-mid-1960s quartet period and a key document of the pre-Keith Jarrett edition of his working band, admired for its lyricism, its blues feeling, and the chemistry among its four musicians.

The Label

The labels are the red Columbia two-eye design, with the circular "eye" logo boxed once on each side of the spindle, white "360 SOUND" arc text at the bottom rim, and "MONO" printed beneath the title - no deep groove ring is present around the label. This two-eye, 360 Sound mono design is the label Columbia used on its mono jazz LPs in the mid-1960s, before the company moved to later orange and yellow label treatments, and it places this copy in that original mono pressing era for the catalogue number, ahead of any later repress or reissue carrying a different label design.

Fidelity

The mono mix was cut for Columbia's original 1965 release and pressed at the company's Pitman, New Jersey plant, a facility generally regarded as solid but unexceptional among period Columbia pressings. Collectors on the forums consider the original mono cut the most natural-sounding way to hear this quartet's interplay, prizing it over the simultaneously issued stereo edition, which some find thinner in the guitar-bass-drums blend. No audiophile remastering of this title has achieved wide recognition, so this original mono pressing sits at or near the top of the fidelity hierarchy for the album, alongside other copies of the same 1965 two-eye mono pressing.

Notes

This is an original-era 1965 Columbia mono pressing of Of Course, Of Course, identified by the red two-eye Columbia labels with "360 SOUND" mono text at the bottom. The runout reads P111028-2C on one side and XLP 111029-1B on the other, a stamper pairing distinct from the 2D/1A pairing also found on copies of this mono pressing, indicating a different stamper from the same original mono pressing run rather than a later reissue. The two-eye label design and the Pitman-era mono mastering both place it within the 1965 first-pressing window. No later label design elements (orange/yellow Columbia labels, record-club text, or digital remaster credits) are present.
