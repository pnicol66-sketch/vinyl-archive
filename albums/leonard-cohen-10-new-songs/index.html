The Album

Ten New Songs marked Leonard Cohen's first album of original material in nine years, arriving after a long stretch spent largely in seclusion at a Zen monastery on Mount Baldy, California, where he was ordained as a Buddhist monk. The record was written and largely performed as a close collaboration with singer-songwriter Sharon Robinson, who co-wrote every song, sang on nearly every track, and built the spare, synthesizer-based arrangements around Cohen's voice. Thematically the songs turn on aging, mortality, devotion, and withdrawal from the world, delivered in the low, weathered baritone Cohen had settled into by this point in his career. Coming after a near-decade absence from recording, the album was received by critics and longtime fans as a quiet, mature return rather than a dramatic reinvention, reaffirming Cohen's standing as one of popular song's most literate and enduring voices. Its hushed, minimalist production and the deep partnership with Robinson would shape the sound of Cohen's subsequent work for the rest of his career.

The Label

The side labels on this copy are a light-blue to turquoise ground with the boxed Columbia "eye" logo at right and the Sony Music Entertainment Inc./"Columbia" copyright rim text dated 2001, matching the Columbia corporate label design used from the early 1990s onward. The copyright line reads "©/℗ 2001," which is the earliest year this specific label design could have been printed, so this copy cannot predate 2001. This label design and catalogue number (C 85953/AL-BL 85953) place the copy in the original US Columbia LP issue window rather than any of the later European, club, or audiophile reissue lines that carry different catalogue numbers.

Fidelity

The album was mixed at two Los Angeles-area studios and mastered at Gateway Mastering, a modern digital-era mastering chain typical of major-label releases from this period rather than an analog audiophile cut. Collectors generally regard this as a well-produced but unremarkable-sounding standard vinyl pressing, with the digital source limiting the sense of 'vinyl magic' some seek in Cohen's earlier analog-era LPs. No audiophile reissue series (Mobile Fidelity, Analogue Productions, etc.) exists for this title; the 2009 Music On Vinyl and 2018 remastered pressings are the only widely discussed alternative cuts, and neither is considered a dramatic upgrade over this original.
