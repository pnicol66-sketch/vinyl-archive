The Album

The Man With The Horn marked Miles Davis's return to recording after nearly six years of self-imposed retirement following chronic illness, substance problems and the exhausted, abrasive electric music of his mid-1970s live albums. Sessions were pieced together across roughly a year, mixing newly written material from a young Chicago-based writing and production team - singer-arranger Randy Hall and keyboardist Robert Irving III - with tracks cut by Davis's reconvened touring band, featuring guitarists Mike Stern and Barry Finnerty, bassist Marcus Miller in one of his earliest high-profile studio dates, drummer Al Foster and soprano saxophonist Bill Evans. The record is deliberately two-sided in character: one half leans toward slick, synthesizer-laced funk vamps built around Hall and Irving's songs, the other returns to long-form, horn-led improvisation closer to Davis's earlier electric style, including the elegiac ‘Ursula’ and the brass-coloured ‘Aïda’. As Davis's first new studio album of the 1980s it reintroduced him to a changed musical landscape and to a new generation of sidemen who would shape his sound through the decade, most notably Miller, who went on to co-produce and compose much of Davis's subsequent catalogue. Critical reaction on release was mixed, with some critics welcoming any new Davis music after the long silence and others finding the record uneven and tentative compared to his earlier landmark work. In hindsight it is regarded less as a creative peak than as a necessary and historically important bridge, the record that re-launched one of jazz's most consequential careers and set the stage for the more fully realised comeback albums that followed.

The Label

Both sides carry a flat red Columbia label with yellow ‘COLUMBIA’ lettering repeated around the rim, a boxed circular eye logo set between each repetition, and no deep groove ring around the spindle hole. This flat, repeating-rim-text red design was Columbia's standard domestic label from the 1970s into the mid-1980s, and the ©/℗ 1981 CBS Inc. line printed on the label dates this pressing to 1981 or later. The design and date place the record as a standard-issue US Columbia pressing from the album's release year, without further distinguishing it among Columbia's several pressing plants active at the time.

Fidelity

The album was mastered at CBS Studios New York on the CBS DisComputer digital mastering and EQ system rather than through a conventional analog tape-to-lacquer chain, and collectors on the forums commonly describe the resulting sound as somewhat thin, hissy and dynamically flat regardless of specific pressing. Quality varies copy to copy within the 1981 US and contemporaneous European pressings, which are generally regarded as the best-sounding option for this title since no audiophile specialist reissue (Mobile Fidelity, Analogue Productions, Music Matters or similar) has been produced for this album. A later clear-vinyl reissue sourced from a newer digital remaster exists but is rated inconsistently by collectors and is not considered to surpass a clean original pressing such as this one.

Notes

1981 Columbia FC 36790 stereo pressing on the standard red Columbia label used across Columbia's several US plants that year; nothing on the label itself narrows the copy to one specific plant. The runout reads AL 36790-1E / BL-36790-1F ‘COLUMBIA NY’, confirming CBS Studios New York mastering but omitting the plant letter and full stamper suffix that would pin down the exact pressing run, and the two sides carry different stamper-letter prefixes (1E and 1F), indicating a mixed early stamper pairing rather than a single uniform run. The jacket carries a printed UPC barcode, a feature not typically present on the earliest Columbia jackets of this catalogue number, which points to this specific jacket being from a slightly later print run of the same 1981 release rather than the very first production. As a mass-produced major-label title with no deep groove and no promotional markings, this is a routine domestic pressing whose value rests on condition rather than on plant rarity.
