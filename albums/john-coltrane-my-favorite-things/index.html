The Album

My Favorite Things was recorded at Atlantic's New York studios across three sessions in October 1960, shortly after Coltrane left Miles Davis's band to lead his own quartet full-time. It is most famous for introducing Coltrane's soprano saxophone to the wider public, an instrument he had only recently taken up, on a radically reworked reading of the Rodgers and Hammerstein title tune that stretched a show tune into a hypnotic, modal vamp far removed from its Broadway origins. The record paired that landmark performance and a soprano take on Gershwin's "Summertime" with two tenor ballads, showing Coltrane's dual command of both horns within a single session. Coming directly after Giant Steps, the album marked Coltrane's pivot from dense harmonic "sheets of sound" writing toward the more open, vamp-based modal playing that would define his mid-1960s work. The quartet here - McCoy Tyner, Steve Davis and Elvin Jones - previewed the rhythm section (with Jimmy Garrison soon replacing Davis) that anchored Coltrane's celebrated Impulse! group. Critically and commercially, the title track became one of Coltrane's most recognized recordings and helped popularize the soprano saxophone in jazz for a generation of players who followed. The album is now regarded as a pivotal bridge session in Coltrane's development, standing alongside Giant Steps and Africa/Brass as evidence of an artist reshaping his musical language within a single year.

The Label

The label on this copy is the all-colour "red/plum" Atlantic design: salmon-red top half, magenta/plum bottom half, black print, a white center band reading ATLANTIC with the black box "swirl" logo at right - no deep groove ring is present. This design superseded the earlier black label with the white/multicolor "fan" pinwheel logo that carried Atlantic's first mono pressings of 1361 in 1961; the plum/orange all-colour label ran roughly 1962 through the mid-1960s before pressing-plant identification began appearing on labels around 1965. The catalogue number here is printed plain "1361" with no "SD" prefix and no STEREO wording, confirming mono. This copy is a mono repress issued once Atlantic had moved to the plum label, circa 1962-65, rather than the true 1961 first mono pressing on the black/white-fan label. Deep groove: not present - flat label.

Fidelity

The mono mastering here derives from Atlantic's original in-house lacquers (matrix 11755/11756, "AT" suffix), the same audio source used for the true 1961 first pressing, but cut on later stampers for this repress rather than the earliest deep-groove metalwork. Collectors on the forums generally rate the earliest deep-groove Atlantic mono and stereo pressings of this title as sonically superior, prizing the warmth and immediacy of the vintage tube-era mastering over later pressings and the 1961 stereo issue's Avco-cut lacquers, which some find thinner. Modern 180g reissues (e.g. Rhino/Atlantic reissue series and Atlantic-catalogue audiophile pressings) offer quieter, more consistent surfaces but are sourced from later-generation tape transfers and generally rank below a clean original-lacquer pressing for tonal immediacy. This copy, as a later flat-label mono repress, sits in the middle of that hierarchy: same mono mix and lacquer lineage as the first pressing, but not the earliest, most prized deep-groove stamper.
