The Album

Bluesey Burrell was recorded on September 14, 1962 at Rudy Van Gelder's Englewood Cliffs studio, one of a run of relaxed, blues-and-ballad-centered sessions Prestige cut for its mood-music Moodsville imprint. Guitarist Kenny Burrell, already established as one of the most lyrical and blues-steeped voices on the instrument, is paired here with tenor giant Coleman Hawkins, the father of the jazz tenor saxophone, for four of the seven tracks, while the remaining pieces feature Burrell's quartet alone with pianist Tommy Flanagan, bassist Major Holley, drummer Eddie Locke and conguero Ray Barretto. The program mixes standards ("I Thought About You," "Out of This World," "Guilty") with a Latin-tinged original, "Tres Talbras," and two Burrell-penned blues, reflecting the loose, unhurried, late-afternoon session described in the original liner notes by Robert Levin. The record arrived at a productive moment for Burrell, sandwiched between other Prestige/Moodsville dates and just months before his best-known Blue Note album, Midnight Blue; it captures him at his most relaxed and unhurried, favoring warm tone and economical phrasing over technical display. Hawkins, for his part, was enjoying a late-career renaissance in the early 1960s, and his appearances here are treated by critics as a highlight, his still-vital, breathy authority anchoring the more uptempo cuts. Though a modest, low-key release even by Moodsville's mood-music standards, the album has endured as a favorite among deep soul-jazz collectors for its unpretentious blues feeling and the rare studio pairing of Burrell and Hawkins, and it was praised on release, drawing a four-star review in Down Beat.

The Label

The side labels are the standard green Moodsville design with silver print, MOODSVILLE banner, the 203 S. Washington Ave., Bergenfield, N.J. address, and "HIGH FIDELITY" band at the base, consistent with Moodsville's 1961-64 label design. This copy carries the label catalogue number MVLP 29 (mono) rather than MVST 29, and the vinyl is deep-groove on both sides, distinguishing it from the flat, no-deep-groove mono pressing of the same label misprint. The label's track A4 is printed "MONTUNO BLUES," a Moodsville-era typo also seen on the non-deep-groove mono pressing and carried through into the 2016 Analogue Productions reissue, so the misspelling alone does not separate pressings - it is the physical deep groove that dates this copy to Moodsville's earliest (1961-63) mono manufacturing run, before the deep-groove feature was phased out industry-wide by the mid-1960s.

Fidelity

Rudy Van Gelder recorded and cut this session himself; the mono lacquer carries his signature warm, present, slightly forward jazz sound typical of his early-60s Prestige/Moodsville dates, and the deep-groove pressing here represents Moodsville's earliest and most direct cut from that master. Collectors on the forums generally rate these original mono Van Gelder Moodsville/Prestige pressings above the contemporaneous stereo counterpart for tonal cohesion, since the mono mix avoids the hard-panned, less-musical channel separation of the stereo and later stereo-sourced reissues. Within the album's full fidelity hierarchy this original mono Van Gelder pressing sits at or near the top alongside the stereo original, well above the 1968 Prestige PRST 7578 reissue and the OJC/RVG CD remasters, and is generally considered a different but comparably desirable listening experience to the audiophile Analogue Productions 45 RPM and SACD editions (2016/2019), which are sourced from the stereo master and prized for extreme quietness and separation rather than mono cohesion.

Notes

This is the mono Moodsville pressing (label reads MVLP 29, not the stereo MVST 29), with etched "MVLP#29A/B VAN GELDER" runouts and a stamped Van Gelder mark. The deep-groove vinyl places it with the earliest mono manufacturing of this release rather than the later, flat-label mono repress, supporting an early-1963 manufacturing date. The label's "Montuno Blues" misspelling (corrected to "Montono Blues" on the jacket) is a known Moodsville-era printing error and does not itself distinguish pressings, so the deep groove is the decisive tell here.
