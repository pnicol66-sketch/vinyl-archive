The Album

Rushed out in mid-1969 to capitalize on the momentum of 'Nuff Said!, To Love Somebody found Nina Simone turning almost entirely to contemporary songwriters rather than her own compositions, reworking Bob Dylan, the Bee Gees, Pete Seeger and Leonard Cohen through her own dramatic, gospel-and-blues-inflected sensibility. The album arrived at a pivotal, turbulent point in Simone's career, as she moved deeper into overtly political material following the assassinations of Martin Luther King Jr. and the mounting urgency of the civil rights and Black Power movements. "Revolution," co-written with Weldon Irvine, was the record's sole original composition and became a statement of ambivalence about the era's calls for radical change, released as a single that reached the R&B charts. Her cover of the title track, the Bee Gees' "To Love Somebody," became a genuine hit in the UK, following on the success of "Ain't Got No, I Got Life," and cemented Simone's late-1960s reputation as an interpreter capable of transforming pop and folk standards into something starker and more searching. The record captures Simone at the height of her powers as a song stylist, an artist whose piano technique, classical training and jazz phrasing let her treat other writers' songs as vehicles for her own convictions rather than simple covers. Critically the album has been viewed as somewhat transitional and uneven next to Simone's more overtly conceptual work of the period, but individual performances - especially the two-part "Revolution" and her reading of Dylan's "I Shall Be Released" - are frequently singled out and remain staples of Simone compilations and reissue programs. Its legacy endures largely through the strength of these interpretations and its role in Simone's ongoing reinvention as a voice of the movement, bridging her jazz-cabaret roots and her increasingly political, soul-inflected sound of the late 1960s and early 1970s.

The Label

The side labels are the standard orange RCA Victor design with the boxed "RCA" logo at left and "Victor" script at right, no deep-groove-era address text beyond "Radio Corporation of America - Made in U.S.A.", consistent with RCA's late-1960s Dynagroove-era orange labels used across the LSP-4000 series. The rear cover carries the Dynagroove logo and the line "Printed in U.S.A." in the last line of the credit block, which Discogs uses to distinguish this specific US pressing variant from other regional or later covers of the same catalogue number. The runouts read "R" (RCA's Rockaway, NJ pressing plant) with 2S stampers on both sides, matching Discogs release 2186169's Variant 1 runout, placing this among the earliest US stamper generation for LSP-4152 rather than a later 5S-stamped repress.

Fidelity

This is an RCA Dynagroove-era cutting, recorded at RCA's Studio B, New York, and pressed at RCA's own Rockaway, NJ plant; Dynagroove processing was RCA's proprietary equalization/compression system of the era and collectors' opinions on its sonic character are mixed, with some finding it a bit closed-in compared to earlier RCA pressings. No audiophile remaster of this title has superseded the original cut in collector estimation; later reissues (Music On Vinyl 180g, various 2010s-2020s remastered pressings) are generally regarded as competent but not sonic upgrades over a clean original stamper. As an early 2S-stampered US pressing with deep-groove labels, this copy sits at the top of the fidelity hierarchy for the title, ahead of later-stamper US repressings, European (German/UK) pressings, and the modern 180g reissues.
