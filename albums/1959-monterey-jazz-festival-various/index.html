The Album

This LP documents blues shouter Jimmy Witherspoon's now-legendary comeback set at the second Monterey Jazz Festival on October 2, 1959, an appearance that revived his career after a decade of struggling in dingy clubs following his late-1940s R&B hits. Backed initially by the Earl Hines Trio (Hines on piano, Vernon Alley on bass, Mel Lewis on drums), Witherspoon's performance built to such intensity that Roy Eldridge, Coleman Hawkins, Ben Webster, Urbie Green and Woody Herman spontaneously joined in for an extended all-star jam that closed the evening. The set became one of the defining moments in the young festival's history and one of the most celebrated live blues-jazz performances of the era, credited with re-establishing Witherspoon as a major vocal artist for the rest of his career. Witherspoon, who had scored a national hit years earlier with "Ain't Nobody's Business" and had recorded and toured with Jay McShann's band, found in this Monterey set the vindication of his classic blues-shouting style at a moment when rock and roll had eclipsed it commercially. The recording captures the raw electricity of a large festival crowd responding to a mix of blues standards and jump numbers, with the assembled horn giants trading solos behind Witherspoon's vocals. Critically the performance has long been cited as a highlight of Monterey's early years and a key document in Witherspoon's rediscovery narrative, cementing his reputation as one of the great blues singers to emerge from the swing and jump-blues tradition.

The Label

The side labels are burnt-orange with the Everest "book and guitar" Archive of Folk & Jazz Music logo, rim text reading ARCHIVE OF FOLK & JAZZ MUSIC, and matrix stamped simply FS 239 A / FS 239 B, matching the Everest Archive series design used from roughly 1969 into the early 1970s after Everest reorganized its jazz/folk reissues under this house imprint. Discogs documents runouts for this catalog number with hand-etched mastering dates by engineer Pete Helffrich at ARC (Allied Record Company) in both mid-1971 and January 1972, indicating at least two pressing runs of this reissue; the collector's typed transcription ("FS 239A"/"FS 239 B") does not include a date suffix, so it cannot be pinned to one of those two specific stamper runs from the data given. Either way, this Everest Archive pressing is a reissue - with the two sides in reversed order - of the original c.1959-60 HiFi Jazz release of the same Monterey Festival tape, so it sits below the HiFi Jazz original in the collecting hierarchy despite the more common/available stereo pressing.

Fidelity

Everest was known for recording live jazz dates on 35mm magnetic film for superior stereo fidelity, and this Monterey set was captured with genuine stereo microphone placement rather than an electronic rechannel job, giving it more natural stage imaging than many contemporaneous mono festival tapes. This particular pressing, however, is a budget-line early-1970s Everest reissue mastered by Pete Helffrich at Allied Record Company (ARC), a plant/mastering combination associated with adequate but unspectacular commercial quality rather than audiophile-grade cutting. Collector forums do not treat this title as an audiophile reference pressing; the original HiFi Jazz LP is generally considered closer to the source tape and preferred by purists, while this Everest reissue is valued mainly for cost and availability rather than sonic superiority.

Notes

This is the Everest Records "Archive of Folk & Jazz Music" stereo reissue, catalog FS-239, not the original c.1959-60 HiFi Jazz LP of the same performance. The orange Archive-series label design and simple FS 239 A/B matrix format match Everest's early-1970s reissue program, and Discogs documents Pete Helffrich/ARC mastering stamps dated 1971 and January 1972 for this catalog number. Discogs also notes the two sides were sequenced in reverse order compared to the original HiFi Jazz release, a useful identifying trait for this pressing. The collector's transcription lacks the ARC date suffix, so the exact stamper run (1971 vs. January 1972) cannot be confirmed from the data given.
