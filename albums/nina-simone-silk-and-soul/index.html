The Album

Silk & Soul arrived in 1967 as the follow-up to Nina Simone Sings the Blues, cementing the direction of her mid-RCA years: original songs of social conscience and personal reckoning sitting alongside pop and soundtrack covers, all filtered through her singular blend of jazz, gospel, blues and classical training. The album is best remembered for "I Wish I Knew How It Would Feel to Be Free," written by Billy Taylor and adopted by Simone as a civil-rights anthem, and for "Turning Point," one of several songs here addressing racial identity and childhood with unflinching directness. Several tracks were contributed by people in her personal orbit - her husband and manager Andy Stroud wrote "Love O' Love," and Morris Bailey Jr. supplied "Go to Hell" - giving the record an unusually intimate, workshop feel even as it reached for the pop charts with covers like Bacharach and David's "The Look of Love" and the Association's "Cherish." Arranger-conductor Sammy Lowe wrapped the songs in lush, radio-friendly orchestration that softened none of Simone's conviction. The album arrived at a pivotal moment in Simone's career, as she was increasingly outspoken in the civil rights movement and moving away from strict jazz-club repertoire toward a broader, message-driven soul sound. Critically it has endured as one of the strongest latter-day statements of her RCA period, prized by soul and jazz collectors alike for the way it balances commercial polish with the uncompromising voice that later earned her the title "High Priestess of Soul."

The Label

This copy carries the grey-background RCA Victor label with the colour Nipper-and-gramophone logo centred at top, white arched "RCA VICTOR" rim text, "STEREO DYNAGROOVE" banner at the foot, and visible deep groove on both sides - the design RCA used for its Dynagroove stereo LPs from 1965 until the switch to the flat orange "block-letter RCA" label in late 1968. This is the first stereo issue of LSP-3837, with the mono LPM-3837 sharing the same label era; a later pressing of this catalogue number was reissued on the orange label once RCA retired the Nipper trademark. The deep-groove pressing, grey Dynagroove label and matrix prefixes UPRS-4299/4300 place this copy in that 1967 first-stereo-pressing window, ahead of the orange-label repress.

Fidelity

Recorded at RCA's Studio B in New York with Ray Hall engineering, this is a standard RCA Dynagroove-era stereo cut rather than an audiophile mastering; Dynagroove's signal processing draws mixed opinions among collectors on the forums, some finding the top end slightly brittle compared with straight analogue RCA cuts of the same period. As an original grey-label stereo pressing it sits above the later orange-label domestic repress and any Japanese or European licensed pressings in collector esteem, but below the audiophile ORG Music 180g 45rpm 2LP reissue, which was cut for improved dynamics and quiet surfaces and is generally regarded as the sonic reference for this title, at the cost of the flatter Dynagroove-era tonal balance being altered.
