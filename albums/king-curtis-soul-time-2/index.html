This Pressing

Budget-line reissue (c. 1973-76) - Up Front/Springboard International repackage of early-1960s King Curtis Tru-Sound/Enjoy sax sides, not an original pressing of any individual track.

The Album

King Curtis, born Curtis Ousley in Fort Worth, Texas, was one of the defining tenor saxophonists of R&B and early soul, celebrated for his "yakety" honking style heard on hits by the Coasters and countless New York session dates of the late 1950s. The tracks gathered here - "Soul Twist," "Big Dipper," "Camp Meetin'," "What'd I Say," and companions - stem from his early-1960s instrumental sax-combo recordings for Bobby Robinson's Harlem-based Enjoy/Tru-Sound labels, cut in the wake of his Prestige jazz dates and just before his mid-1960s Capitol and Atco/Atlantic years. "Soul Twist" itself was a genuine national hit, riding the twist-dance craze to the top of the R&B charts in 1962 and becoming one of Curtis's signature instrumentals. These sides capture Curtis at a pivotal, transitional moment - moving from raw honking rock and roll saxophone toward the funkier, groove-driven instrumental soul he would perfect later in the decade - and they helped cement the tenor sax as a lead voice in soul music rather than a mere backing texture. The music's blend of gospel-tinged organ grooves, twist-era backbeats, and Curtis's blistering solos made it a favorite on jukeboxes and in dance clubs, and it remains prized by soul and R&B collectors as some of Curtis's most purely danceable, unpretentious work. Critically, Curtis's catalog from this era is regarded as foundational to instrumental soul-jazz, bridging 1950s rock and roll sax and the Memphis/Muscle Shoals soul sound he would later champion as a bandleader and session ace for Aretha Franklin and others.

The Label

The side labels are solid red with a two-word "UP FRONT" logo (stylized wedge lettering) at top, catalogue number UPF-157 and matrix SLX-00347/00348 at left, "SIDE A/B - STEREO" at right, and rim text reading "A PRODUCT OF SPRINGBOARD INTERNATIONAL RECORDS, INC., RAHWAY, NEW JERSEY 07065." No deep groove is visible. Up Front was a subsidiary of Springboard International, a New Jersey/Los Angeles budget-reissue operation that recycled licensed masters (here, King Curtis's early-1960s Tru-Sound/Enjoy sax instrumentals) onto cheap repackaged LPs through the 1970s under a rotating cast of titles and covers. The Rahway, NJ address and this specific catalogue design place the pressing squarely in Springboard's mid-1970s budget-line output, not an original or first-issue pressing of any of the individual tracks.

Fidelity

The source masters are early-1960s mono studio recordings for Bobby Robinson's Enjoy/Tru-Sound operation; the "STEREO" billing on this Up Front pressing is very likely an electronic rechanneling job typical of Springboard International's budget reissue line rather than a true stereo master. Mastering and pressing quality on Springboard/Up Front product is generally utilitarian - thinner vinyl, minimal EQ care, and audible surface noise are common collector complaints (see general Springboard/Trip discussion on forums.stevehoffman.tv). Within the fidelity hierarchy for these King Curtis performances, the original Tru-Sound/Enjoy mono 45s and LP represent the closest, most faithful cut to the master tape; this Up Front stereo repackage sits well below that, and any later CD compilations sourced from cleaner tape transfers likely surpass it sonically as well.

Notes

This is the Up Front (Springboard International) UPF-157 budget repackage "Soul Time," not a first pressing of any of its constituent recordings. The plain red "UP FRONT" labels with Rahway, NJ rim text and matrix codes SLX-00347/00348 match Springboard's mid-1970s budget-line design, used to recycle licensed masters (here, Curtis's 1961-62 Tru-Sound/Enjoy instrumentals, including the hit "Soul Twist") under repeatedly recycled titles and jacket art. There is no deep groove, Van Gelder-type stamp, or other first-press marker to look for since this is a compilation reissue rather than an original studio LP; the stereo billing on masters recorded in mono-era sessions strongly suggests electronic rechanneling.
