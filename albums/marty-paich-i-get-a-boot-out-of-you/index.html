The Album

Recorded across three sessions in Hollywood in the summer of 1959, this is arranger-pianist Marty Paich's tribute to swing-era standards refracted through the tightly voiced, mid-sized big-band sound he had pioneered on his celebrated Mel Torme collaborations. Paich assembled a crack roster of West Coast studio and jazz players for the date, most notably altoist Art Pepper (on loan courtesy of Contemporary Records) and vibraphonist Victor Feldman (courtesy of Columbia), alongside trumpeter Jack Sheldon, pianist Russ Freeman and drummer Mel Lewis, giving the session an unusually strong solo bench for what was billed as a swing repertory record. The book leans on Ellington (four of the eight tunes, including "Moanin'" via Jon Hendricks's vocalese lyric turned instrumental and the Ellington-Strayhorn staples "Warm Valley" and "Cottontail"), plus Cole Porter's "Love For Sale" and other standards, each reworked with Paich's harmonically adventurous, contrapuntal charts rather than played as straight repertory. The record arrived at the height of Paich's run as one of the era's most in-demand jazz arrangers, sandwiched between his own Broadway Bit and further Warner Bros. dates, and it stands as a prime example of the West Coast "cool" school's application of modern arranging technique to older material. Critically it has endured as a favorite among cool-jazz collectors less for chart innovation alone than for the quality of the blowing Paich drew out of his soloists, Pepper's turns in particular being singled out by later listeners as some of his best studio work outside his own Contemporary leader dates. It remains a benchmark of late-1950s West Coast big-band jazz and a key entry in both Paich's and Pepper's discographies.

The Label

The side labels photographed here show the classic Warner Bros. gold/champagne shield design: a single black-outlined-red WB shield at 12 o'clock, no additional arc band beyond the plain "VITAPHONIC ... STEREO ... LONG PLAY" rim wording, catalogue number WS 1349 with the matrix reference (S 38427)/(S 38428) printed just below it, and a deep groove ring visible just inside the printed area on both sides. This is the gold-label design Warner Bros. used from the label's 1958 launch through the early-to-mid 1960s, before the switch to the black corporate "WB" shield label with color bands later in the decade. The stereo catalogue prefix WS, the S 38427/S 38428 matrix pair, and the U.S./GB Vitaphonic-groove patent numbers etched in the runout are consistent with Warner's earliest stereo pressing run for this 1959 title, placing this copy at the front of the WS 1349 pressing hierarchy - ahead of the later black-label repress and well ahead of the 1981 Trend/Discovery DS-829 reissue (different matrix, KM 6267 A/B DS-829) or any modern gold-label facsimile reissue, both of which use different label art and matrices entirely.

Fidelity

This is an all-analog late-1950s Warner Bros. Hollywood cut, sourced directly from the original 1959 3-track session tapes with no digital step in the chain; the deep-groove gold-label stamping and S 38427/S 38428 R2 matrix indicate an early stamper generation rather than a worn late repress. Collectors on forums such as Steve Hoffman's rate original Warner Bros. jazz stereo pressings of this vintage as solid, punchy, if occasionally hard-edged in the trumpets typical of early Warner stereo engineering, with the mono W 1349 pressing sometimes preferred by purists for smoother blend of the horn section. There is no audiophile reissue (Analogue Productions, Music Matters, Mobile Fidelity, etc.) of this title to compare against; the only alternate cuts are the 1981 Trend/Discovery reissue (a later, several-generation-removed lacquer) and later gold-label facsimile pressings, both of which rank below this early-stamper stereo original in the fidelity hierarchy.

Notes

1959 Warner Bros. WS 1349 stereo - original first pressing. Gold/champagne shield label with deep groove and the S 38427/S 38428 R2 matrix pair (visible under the catalogue number on the labels) place this within Warner's earliest stereo run for the title, ahead of the later black-label WB repress and well ahead of the 1981 Trend/Discovery DS-829 reissue, which carries an entirely different etched matrix. The Vitaphonic patent-number runout text is the era-correct etching for Warner's late-1950s stereo LPs. No promo or club markings are present; this is a standard commercial stereo first issue.
