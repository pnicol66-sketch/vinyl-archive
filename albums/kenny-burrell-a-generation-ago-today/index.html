The Album

Recorded in 1966 for Verve under producer Creed Taylor, A Generation Ago Today finds guitarist Kenny Burrell paying tribute to the swing era, reworking tunes closely associated with Benny Goodman and the Charlie Christian small-group sessions of the late 1930s. Burrell, one of the definitive post-bop jazz guitarists of the era known for his work with Blue Note and his own sideman dates for Miles Davis and John Coltrane, here reaches back to the music that shaped his own playing, framing Christian's pioneering electric-guitar vocabulary within a modern small-group setting. The presence of alto saxophonist Phil Woods lends a bop-inflected voice to swing-era staples like 'Stompin' at the Savoy' and 'Rose Room,' while the closing quartet track 'Wholly Cats' strips the band down for a leaner, more intimate look at the repertoire. The album sits in the middle of Burrell's prolific Verve period, when he moved fluidly between mainstream jazz dates and more commercial crossover sessions, and it stands as one of his more overtly historical, backward-glancing statements. Contemporary and later critical assessment has generally treated it as a solid, respectful genre exercise rather than a landmark, valued chiefly for Burrell's fluent, blues-rooted guitar lines and the quality of his supporting cast. It remains a minor but appealing entry in the guitarist's substantial 1960s discography, of most interest to collectors tracing his stylistic range across the decade.

The Label

The label is the grey 'boomerang/dart' Verve design used after MGM's ownership of Verve, with rim text reading 'MGM RECORDS - A DIVISION OF METRO-GOLDWYN-MAYER, INC. - MADE IN U.S.A.' The catalogue number is printed as plain 'V-8656 (MG-164/165)' with no '6' prefix, which is Verve's standard mono designation (V6- denotes stereo); this places the copy in the mono run of the 1967 first pressing rather than the stereo V6-8656 edition. This MGM-era grey label with the dart logo dates to Verve's mid-to-late-1960s design window, consistent with the LP's 1967 release, and the plain V- number together with the Van Gelder-stamped runouts supports an original mono first-pressing placement rather than a later reissue.

Fidelity

Both sides were mastered by Rudy Van Gelder, as shown by the 'VAN GELDER' etchings in the runouts, giving a warm, well-balanced cut typical of his Verve-era jazz work for Creed Taylor. This is an original-era US pressing rather than a later reissue, and collectors on the forums generally regard these original Van Gelder-cut Verve LPs as sonically preferable to the later Japanese Polydor/Verve reissues (MV-2115, MV-4006, 20MJ-0091) and to the 1986/2004 digital remasters, which tend to sound brighter and more compressed. Within this title's fidelity hierarchy, this mono first pressing ranks at or near the top, alongside its stereo (V6-8656) sibling, ahead of all Japanese vinyl reissues and CD/digital remasters.
