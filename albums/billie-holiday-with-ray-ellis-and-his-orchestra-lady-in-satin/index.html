The Album

Lady in Satin was recorded February 18-21, 1958 at Columbia's 30th Street Studio in New York, produced by Irving Townsend - Billie Holiday's last completed album for Columbia and the penultimate studio statement of her life, released little more than a year before her death in July 1959. Holiday herself pushed for the project, insisting she wanted to record with Ray Ellis's lush strings-and-voices arrangements, a deliberately un-jazzy setting she chose over the objections of purists; she called it the favorite record she ever made. Her voice, weathered by decades of hard living, had lost its youthful suppleness, and critics at the time were divided over pairing that frayed instrument with Ellis's satin-smooth orchestra. But the emotional directness of performances like 'I'm a Fool to Want You' (co-written by Sinatra, who reportedly left the studio in tears at the power of her rendition) and 'You've Changed' transformed frailty into devastating expressive force. The album reframed Holiday not as a swinger but as one of the great dramatic interpreters of American song. It has since been inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame and is widely regarded as one of the essential vocal albums in jazz - the late-career companion piece to her 1930s-40s prime.

The Label

This copy carries the red Columbia six-eye label (six walking-eye logos ringing the perimeter) with deep groove on both sides, 'NONBREAKABLE' beneath the catalog number, and rim text reading 'COLUMBIA ... MARCAS REG. ... T.M. MADE IN U.S.A.' with no CBS overprint. The six-eye design ran from mid-1955 to about 1962; around 1961-62 a small CBS overprint was added beneath the Columbia name marking repressings, and the two-eye '360 Sound' label replaced the six-eye entirely after 1962. The label-printed matrices x'Lp'43015/43016 match the first-issue mono. This copy therefore sits at the top of the mono pressing hierarchy - the earliest retail configuration of the June 1958 release.

Fidelity

Recorded at Columbia's 30th Street Studio with its famed natural reverb, the original six-eye mono is prized by collectors for a warm, close, emotionally immediate vocal presence that puts Holiday front and center; many on the Steve Hoffman forums consider it the definitive way to hear the album despite the limited bandwidth of 1958 mono. Six-eye-era Columbia pressings are generally quiet and well cut. The original stereo CS 8048 six-eye is the audiophile grail but spreads the voice in a more diffuse orchestral field. Among modern reissues, the Analogue Productions editions (stereo 33 and the 2023 Ryan Smith/Sterling-cut 45rpm 2xLP pressed at QRP) outresolve the original, while Speakers Corner's mono reissue is the closest modern stand-in for this pressing. This copy's G-grade vinyl, however, will heavily compromise any of these inherent sonic virtues.

Notes

1958 Columbia CL 1157 mono - original first pressing configuration. Red six-eye deep-groove labels with T.M. in the rim print and no CBS overprint place it in the pre-1961/62 six-eye window, and this exact label variant is documented as the original mono issue; the boxed 'Guaranteed High-Fidelity' cover logo is consistent with the June 1958 release. Label-printed matrices x'Lp'43015/43016 match the first-issue mono. No dead-wax photos were supplied, so the pressing plant (Pitman, Terre Haute, etc.) cannot be pinned - a plant stamp in the runout would settle that, though it does not change the first-pressing verdict.
