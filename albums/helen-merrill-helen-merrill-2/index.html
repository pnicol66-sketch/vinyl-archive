The Album

Recorded over two sessions in late December 1954 at Fine Sound Studios in New York, this debut studio album introduced vocalist Helen Merrill to the jazz world in the company of trumpet prodigy Clifford Brown, with arrangements and conducting by a young Quincy Jones making one of his earliest marks on record. Merrill, whose hushed, breathy, deeply intimate delivery set her apart from the more forceful jazz singers of her era, found in this quiet, spacious date the ideal showcase for her phrasing, particularly on the ballads "Don't Explain" and "What's New," the latter later singled out by Jones as one of the most compelling vocal performances he had arranged. Brown, only months away from his death in a 1956 car accident, plays with the lyrical warmth and melodic invention that made him one of the defining trumpet voices of hard bop, and his obbligato lines behind Merrill's vocals are treated as equal partners in the conversation rather than mere accompaniment. The album occupies a pivotal place in Merrill's career as the record that established her reputation as a singer's singer, prized by musicians and cognoscenti for her restraint and intonation rather than for pop crossover appeal. Within the vocal jazz canon, the record is often cited alongside the best work of the era's ballad singers for its unhurried intimacy and its integration of top-tier instrumental soloists into a vocal date. Critical reception over the decades has been consistently glowing, with the session's chemistry between Merrill and Brown frequently singled out as one of the great vocalist-instrumentalist pairings in jazz history, securing the album's lasting stature as an essential and influential title.

The Label

This copy carries the blue EmArcy label with the large silver "drummer painting a record" logo positioned at top, a bordering silver-toned rim text reading "LONG PLAYING HIGH FIDELITY," and deep-groove construction - the hallmarks of the earliest EmArcy pressing of this title. The matrix prefix "YMG" (YMG 36006A/B) on the collector's runout matches the Discogs variant documented as the true first mono pressing with the larger logo and silver rim, distinguishing it from the circa 1956-58 pressing that dropped the silver rim and shrank the logo. EmArcy's blue drummer-logo design with deep groove was used only in this earliest window (1955-56) before Mercury simplified the label art later in the decade. This placement puts the copy at the top of the pressing hierarchy for MG-36006 - the true first release, not a subsequent stamper generation of the same era design.

Fidelity

This is an all-analog first-generation cut, mastered directly from the original session tapes at the dawn of EmArcy's high-fidelity era; the deep-groove pressing and YMG matrix place it ahead of every later stamper and reissue of this title. Collectors on forums.stevehoffman.tv and jazz-specialist sites consistently rate the original EmArcy pressings of this session as sonically superior to the later Mercury/Philips reissues and the Japanese Trio pressings, prizing the warmth and immediacy of the vocal and trumpet on this early stamper generation. The 2020 Analogue Productions APJ 127 200g reissue, mastered by Kevin Gray from the analog tapes, is well regarded for quiet, clean surfaces and improved bass extension but is generally viewed by purists as a fine modern alternative rather than a replacement for a good first pressing. Within the album's fidelity hierarchy, this deep-groove YMG first pressing sits at the top, ahead of later 1950s/60s Mercury reissues, 1970s Japanese pressings, and CD editions, with only the Analogue Productions LP as a serious modern competitor for those unconcerned with owning an original.

Notes

1955 EmArcy MG-36006 mono - true first pressing. Blue "drummer" label with the larger EmArcy logo, silver rim text, and deep groove match the earliest documented Discogs variant, and the YMG-prefixed matrix (YMG 36006A MF2 / YMG 36006B MF1) confirms the same stamper family as that first-issue variant, differing only in stamper suffix (A1 here vs. B2/B1 on the cataloged copy) - a normal stamper progression within the same first pressing, not a different issue. The blue-printed back cover and absence of any later Mercury or Trio Japan reissue markers further support this being the original US release.
