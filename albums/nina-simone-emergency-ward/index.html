The Album

Recorded live at Fort Dix and other venues in late 1971, Emergency Ward! is one of Nina Simone's most overtly topical statements, built as a song cycle responding to the Vietnam War and the era's social upheaval rather than a conventional set of standards. The album is anchored by a side-long medley that transforms George Harrison's 'My Sweet Lord' into a gospel-inflected incantation, joined to 'Today Is a Killer', a poem by David Nelson that Simone set to music, its choir and polyrhythmic backing turning devotional material into protest testimony. Simone, already established as a civil-rights-era voice through songs like 'Mississippi Goddam' and 'Four Women', used the record to extend that activism into the anti-war movement at a point when her RCA tenure was winding down. Side two closes with a cover of the Harrison-penned 'Isn't It a Pity', bookending the album's reliance on Harrison material as vehicles for meditation on suffering and redemption. Critically the record has long divided listeners - admired for its ambition and live-in-the-moment intensity, but seen by some as one of Simone's more challenging, sermon-like RCA releases compared to her more song-based albums. It remains a key document of Simone's political and spiritual turn in the early 1970s and of the era's fusion of rock covers, gospel choir, and protest song.

The Label

This copy's Side 1 and Side 2 labels are the orange RCA Victor design with the outlined white 'RCA' logo at left and 'Victor' in script at right, black/brown text, and the 'dynaflex' wordmark plus '(P) 1972 RCA Records' at the bottom - the standard early-1970s US RCA Victor stereo LP label, used from RCA's 1968 Dynaflex introduction into the mid-1970s before the black 'Victor' label replaced it around 1976-77. There is no deep groove and no earlier orange/black 'nipper' design present, so this is not a 1950s/60s-style label carryover. Matrix suffixes BPRS-5348-4S / BPRS-5349-6S with 'A10R' / 'A16R' stamper codes place this squarely as a first-issue-window 1972 US stereo pressing from the Rockaway plant, rather than the later French, Spanish, or reissue pressings that share the LSP-4757 number.

Fidelity

This is a standard early-1970s RCA Dynaflex pressing rather than an audiophile cut; Dynaflex vinyl of the era is thinner and more prone to warping than earlier RCA pressings, though sound quality on clean copies is generally considered solid for a lightly-mixed live recording. Collectors on the forums rate Simone's RCA catalog originals over the CD-era remasters for warmth, though this is a live document with inherent recording limitations rather than a studio hi-fi showcase. The 2006 Speakers Corner 180g reissue (mastered from the RCA tapes) is generally regarded by collectors as the sonic upgrade of choice over both this Dynaflex original and later remastered/coloured-vinyl reissues, though the original retains value for being closest to source lacquers and the most collectible pressing.
