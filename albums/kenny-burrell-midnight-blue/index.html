The Album

Recorded in a single session at Van Gelder Studio on January 8, 1963, Midnight Blue found guitarist Kenny Burrell paring his working group down to a spare, blues-drenched quintet, deliberately omitting piano so his own chording could carry the harmony. Burrell had already built a reputation as one of the most versatile guitarists in jazz, equally comfortable in bebop, soul-jazz and blues settings, and this date leaned hard into the blues and Latin-tinged grooves that were then reshaping Blue Note's commercial sound. Tenor saxophonist Stanley Turrentine's warm, vocalized tone became the record's second voice, while Ray Barretto's conga added a subtle Afro-Cuban lift that distinguished tracks like Chittlins Con Carne and Wavy Gravy from straight 12-bar blues. The album's unhurried, late-night mood and its emphasis on feel over flash made it an immediate favorite with listeners beyond the jazz-specialist audience, and it remains Burrell's most enduring and best-selling Blue Note session. Critics have long cited it as a high-water mark of early-1960s soul jazz, prized for its economy, groove and the chemistry between Burrell and Turrentine. It cemented Burrell's standing as a leader in his own right after years as an in-demand sideman, and its title track and Chittlins Con Carne have become minor blues-guitar standards. The record's blend of blues authenticity and jazz sophistication continues to be cited as a touchstone of the genre.

The Label

The labels on this copy carry the full BLUE NOTE RECORDS INC • NEW YORK USA address with no Liberty-era text, pressed without a deep-groove ring on either side. Plastylite, the New Jersey plant that pressed Blue Note's records, ran deep-groove and flat dies concurrently between 1961 and 1965, so the absence of a groove does not by itself place a copy after the first pressing run. The Plastylite ear in the runout confirms the disc was pressed there, and Plastylite pressed Blue Note's records until mid-1966. Within the BLP 4123 pressing history this is a New York-address pressing of that 1963-1966 span, standing alongside - not necessarily after - the deep-groove copies of the same label design.

Fidelity

The runout carries the same Van Gelder lacquer cut and Plastylite-ear pressing used throughout the New York-label era of this title, so this copy's sound is unchanged from any other NY pressing of 1963-1966 regardless of the presence or absence of a deep groove. Collectors on the forums rate all Van Gelder/ear-stamped mono New York pressings of this record as sonically superior to the later Liberty/United Artists reissues and to the stereo edition, valuing the warmth and impact of the mono cut. Within the fidelity hierarchy for this title, NY-label ear pressings from this window sit together near the top, ahead of the post-1966 Liberty-era and 1970s reissues and the 1985 digital remaster; modern audiophile remasterings use fresh transfers and surpass all vintage mono pressings technically while trading away the vintage mono character.

Notes

This copy carries the original New York address Blue Note labels, with no Liberty-era text, and a flat (non-deep-groove) stamping on both sides.
